## 1. Hackathon Challenge Brief & Toolkit Overview

### Official Track Prompt
* **Track:** The Canada Life Future Me Challenge (1 winning team; \$100 Visa prepaid gift card per team member).
* **Prompt:**
  > Canada Life's research found that many young Canadians are struggling to engage with long-term financial planning due to uncertainty about the world ahead. Your challenge is to design and build a digital solution that helps Gen Z take meaningful action toward their financial future through **motivation, visualization, behavioral nudges, education, or innovation**. Create a solution that **turns uncertainty into confidence** and helps young people see that **the future is still worth planning for**.
* **Sponsor Guidance (Rob Craig, Canada Life):** Explicitly encouraged hackathon teams to look through *The Disappearing Horizon* report and the *Gen Z Workplace Savings Toolkit* for ideas to advance.

### Gen Z Workplace Savings Toolkit Structure (Web Portal)
Canada Life organizes its employer playbook into **6 Modules + Resources**:
1. **Module 1:** The Gen Z engagement context
2. **Module 2:** Assess your current state
3. **Module 3:** Implementation and tools
4. **Module 4:** Considerations for plan design
5. **Module 5:** Moments that matter
6. **Module 6:** Measure and improve

### Generational Definitions Used by Canada Life
* **Gen Z:** 1997 to 2012 (ages 14 to 29 in 2026)
* **Gen Y (Millennials):** 1981 to 1996
* **Gen X:** 1966 to 1980 *(Note: white paper p. 4 lists 1965 to 1980)*
* **Baby Boomers:** 1946 to 1965
* **Builders:** 1928 to 1945

### Target Employer Audience & Business Value
* **Who the toolkit is built for:** HR leaders, benefits professionals, advisors/consultants, business owners, and anyone responsible for workplace retirement and savings plans.
* **Core thesis:** Gen Z's lower rate of long-term saving is **not an awareness problem—it is a relevance problem.** Many understand saving matters; the challenge is helping them see why it matters *for them right now*.
* **Why employers care:** Better attraction and retention of talent, increased perceived value of workplace benefits, improved employee financial well-being, greater employee confidence and engagement, and stronger long-term workforce resilience.

## 2. PDF 1: *The Disappearing Horizon* (Full Research Report, 23 Pages)

* **Full Title:** *The disappearing horizon: Helping Gen Z employees save for a long-term future they can't clearly see*
* **Document ID:** `79-03532 08/26` (Canada Life, in collaboration with Deloitte)

### 2.1 The Core Paradox (Pages 3–4)
* **The Old Story Is Dead:** The traditional retirement narrative ("put a little off each paycheque for 40 years -> retire at 65 to travel, pursue hobbies, see grandkids") feels disconnected or laughable to many Gen Zs, some of whom are unsure the planet will even be livable when they are 65.
* **The Surprising Saving Stat:** A 30-year-old today is actually **saving more and holds a higher RRSP balance** than a 30-year-old did in 2018 *(Canada Life GRS member data via GCAP database)*. Younger workers are informed and starting to save.
* **The Retention Crisis (Headline Stat):** While younger workers are contributing, they are **raiding their retirement accounts early** at much higher rates:
  * Gen Z employees are withdrawing from their RRSPs **12% more than Millennials (Gen Y)** and **42% more than Gen X** did at the same age *(2024 Canada Life Workplace Retirement Savings Member data insights)*.
* **The New Mandate:** The retirement challenge is no longer just helping employees *start* saving—it is **helping them keep their savings invested**.

### 2.2 Research Methodology (Page 5)
Conducted by **Canada Life + Deloitte** as a mixed-methods study exploring how existential threats (climate change, war, cost of living, societal collapse) affect Gen Z's future outlook and long-term savings behaviour:
* **Quantitative Phase:** ~1,900 Canadian respondents (**40% Gen Z**) in a **randomized controlled experimental design**. Measured savings behaviours, financial attitudes, psychological traits, existential threat perceptions, and tested psychological framings/intervention concepts.
* **Qualitative Phase:** **16 one-on-one interviews** with Gen Z Canadians + **2 focus groups** (11 Gen Z corporate employees total) + audit of existing member communications and journeys. Explored emotional/cognitive reactions to messaging and refined interventions before field testing.

### 2.3 Why It Matters Macroeconomically (Pages 6–8)
If Gen Z does not build long-term savings, four societal ripple effects occur:
1. **Decreased capital investment:** Less willingness/ability to invest early.
2. **Increased future dependency on public assistance:** Insufficient personal retirement savings.
3. **Reduced consumer spending in retirement:** Less saved now means less spending power later.
4. **Workforce imbalances:** Working longer out of necessity delays retirement, disrupting career trajectories and skill distribution.

#### Gen Z Workforce Snapshot (Page 7)
* **By 2030:** Gen Z will make up **1 in 4** members of the Canadian workforce.
* **Job Mobility:** Change roles every **1.1 years** on average *(Randstad Report)*.
* **Career Values:** Work-life balance ranks above climbing the corporate ladder; only **6%** say their primary goal is reaching a leadership position.
* **Economic Clout:** Collective global spend projected to reach **\$12 trillion**, overtaking Baby Boomers.

#### Financial Nuance Acknowledged by Canada Life (Page 8)
* Nearly **2 in 3 Canadians** worry about retirement savings.
* Because Gen Z may have potentially longer working careers (a longer time horizon) while facing high debt, elevated interest rates, housing costs, and inflation, **it can actually be financially wiser for many Gen Zs to balance retirement saving with near- and medium-term stability** (paying down high-interest debt, building emergency savings to avoid future borrowing, and saving for a home) rather than focusing exclusively on retirement.

### 2.4 Key Quantitative Findings: Existential Dread & Doomscrolling (Pages 9–12)
For Gen Zs who worry frequently about existential risk, **existential dread matters just as much as cost-of-living pressures** in shaping their attitude toward saving.

#### Existential Risk Metrics (Page 9)
Compared to the rest of the Canadian population, Gen Zs are:
* **70% more likely** to think about the end of humanity several times a week.
* **140% more likely** to talk about the end of humanity several times a week.
* **2x (twice) as likely** to consume dystopian content several times a week.
* **1 in 8 Gen Zs** believe humanity will go extinct in their lifetime.

#### "Soft-Saving" Behaviour (Page 10)
* Supported by Canada Life's *Life in focus* benchmark report: younger savers are adopting **"soft-saving"**—a less rigid mindset that prioritizes short- and medium-term goals and present experiences alongside retirement.
* Instead of locking money in an RRSP, they may spend on present experiences or withdraw from retirement savings to fund something today rather than wait for a future they doubt.

#### The Doomscrolling Feedback Loop (Pages 11–12)
* **Definition:** Compulsive habit of continuously scrolling through news feeds or social media to consume negative, distressing, or alarming content.
* **Chain reaction:** More doomscrolling -> thinking more about the end of the world -> saving less for the future.
* **Daily Exposure:** Gen Zs spend an average of **74 minutes/day** doomscrolling—about **70% more** than the rest of the population (**~4.5 extra hours per week**).
* **Impact on Automation:** **Each additional hour** spent doomscrolling daily is associated with a **4.4% lower probability** of setting up automatic contributions toward long-term savings.
* **Impact of Existential Frequency:** Thinking about the end of the world **just once more per month** is linked to a **4 percentage point decrease** in the likelihood of holding long-term savings products.
* **Cross-Generational Headline (Kelly Peters, Partner & Global Behavioural Economics Leader at Deloitte):** *"No matter the generation, thinking about the end of the world makes people **16% less likely** to own and enrol in long-term savings."*

### 2.5 Qualitative Findings & Verbatim Gen Z Quotes (Pages 10, 13, 16, 21)
* **Fatalism ("Live for now"):**
  * *"I don't know what the future is going to be like... So, I think the most important thing I can do right now is to just live for now."* — Female, 21, Alberta (placed human extinction at 30 years)
  * *"You never know when the world's gonna end. So, do you really want the world to end and not experience the things that you can experience with the money?"* — Female, 24, Quebec (placed human extinction at 30 years)
* **What Fails (Fearmongering & Trend-Bait):**
  * *"It makes things worse because you're telling me that unless I have money... it feeds into those fears."* — Female, 26
  * *"It feels like just following a trend and trying to bait you by throwing some keywords."* — Female, 26
* **What Works (Acknowledgment + Control/Agency):**
  * *"It's very much acknowledging that there's an issue, and then providing those small actionable steps for things we can do right now."* — Female, 26
  * *"I want to know what is within my control. And so I think this gives you [small actions] that you can do and that feels enticing to get started."* — Female, 26
* **The Winning Reframe (Pragmatic Hedging):**
  * *"I don't want to, you know, not save money because I think that humans are going to go extinct. Because, if we don't go extinct, then I'm going to be broke. So, I want to make sure I have enough. Rather be safe than sorry."* — Female, 20, Ontario (placed human extinction at 50 years)

### 2.6 Strategic Frameworks from *The Disappearing Horizon* (Pages 13–22)

#### A. Engage with Authenticity (Pages 13–14)
* Reject fearmongering, dystopian headlines, and superficial pop-culture references.
* Reframe **moments that matter** (enrolment, first bonus, pay raise):
  * Highlight positive benefits while acknowledging Gen Z's reality and short-term priorities differ from past generations.
  * Build trust by clearly showing **when/how savings tools help AND where their limits are**.
  * Position long-term savings as **adaptability across a wide range of possible futures**.

#### B. Small, Actionable Steps — "Something Is Better Than Nothing" (Pages 15–16)
* Clear, straightforward advice is the **antidote to fear-induced paralysis**.
* Concrete examples recommended in the report:
  * Outline the full employer match available, but emphasize how easy it is to change later and that **starting with just 1%** is a great first step.
  * Visualize compounding with relatable small amounts: e.g., **\$20 compounded over 25 years**.
  * Point directly to accessible workplace plan resources (plan financial advice, Gen Z-specific webinars).
* Action plans must: acknowledge concerns without speaking *for* their experience, show why small/flexible steps build resilience, and include immediate actions + contingencies.

#### C. Rethinking Plan Design (Pages 17–18)
1. **Make it easy to start saving:** Because Gen Z changes jobs every 1.1 years, high-friction onboarding kills participation. Reduce barriers immediately.
2. **Balance flexibility with long-term outcomes:** Support multiple savings priorities (short-term resilience + long-term retirement).
3. **Use guidance without removing choice:** Combine strong behavioural defaults (automatic enrolment, smart defaults) with preserved user control and flexibility.

#### D. 5 Practical Pillars ("Pulling It All Together", Pages 19–21)
1. **Make it easy to find good information:** Gen Z follows "finfluencers" and Reddit/investing forums because they *want* to learn *(Canadian Securities Administrators research)*, but social media advice is often unreliable. Employers/providers should deliver **vetted, bite-sized, engaging, life-stage-tailored** education.
2. **Reframe saving for a new generation:** Shift from linear "retire at 65" to **adaptability, options, and resilience**.
3. **Help them save, their way:**
   * Automatic **payroll deductions** + **employer matching**.
   * Offer a **TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account)** alongside the retirement plan (RRSP) so employees can withdraw tax-free for *any* purpose without locking funds away.
   * **Split-bucket routing:** Let employees direct *personal* contributions toward shorter-term savings (TFSA) while *employer* contributions go toward longer-term retirement savings/core products.
4. **Support saving as their needs evolve:**
   * A 25-year-old's goals differ from a 30-year-old's.
   * **Plan review capability:** Prompt employees to re-evaluate allocations every **1 to 3 years** (e.g., shifting from TFSA emergency/short-term focus to long-term retirement as milestones like marriage or parenthood arrive).
   * **Canada Life's Match Allocation Tool:** Backend capability allowing employers to route employer-paid match dollars to long-term retirement accounts while freeing employee-paid contributions to go into a flexible short-term TFSA.
5. **Give them small wins:** Because traditional adulthood milestones (homeownership, linear retirement) feel out of reach, celebrate concrete micro-milestones: building an emergency fund in a TFSA, saving part of a first raise, or committing a set percentage of salary.

#### E. "In a Nutshell" — The 3 Philosophical Anchors (Page 22)
1. **Do not dismiss uncertainty or fears:** Saying "don't worry" or "you're wrong" pushes Gen Z away. Examine our own biases first.
2. **"We don't know" is powerful:** Acknowledging uncertainty creates openness instead of defensiveness and shifts mindset **from doom to preparation**.
3. **Resilience is the bridge between hope and behaviour:** Not blind optimism—equipping people with the capacity to adapt makes preparation feel rational and empowering.

## 3. PDF 2: *Gen Z Workplace Savings Communications Guide* (6 Pages)

* **Full Title:** *Gen Z workplace savings communications guide*
* **Document ID:** `79-03539 08/26`
* **Purpose:** Practical messaging, UX copywriting, and tone manual for emails, intranet posts, enrolment reminders, webinar invites, and in-app nudges. Goal: **support action, not just understanding.** Focus on 1–2 meaningful changes at a time.

### 3.1 The 5 Communication Principles (Page 2)
1. **Acknowledge uncertainty without amplifying fear:** Do not pretend the future is predictable or tell them not to worry. Recognize uncertainty is real, then pivot directly to what they can do today.
2. **Reframe savings as control and flexibility:** Move beyond "retirement at 65." Frame savings as resilience and adaptability across many possible futures.
3. **Make the first step small and immediate:** Action must feel doable. Ask for **one clear step** (e.g., contributing enough to capture full employer matching, or signing up for a short webinar).
4. **Build trust with plain, specific language:** Zero jargon, zero pressure, zero vague promises. Clearly explain what a tool helps with, **what its limits are**, and what to do next.
5. **Use moments that matter:** Anchor messages to real life moments—enrolment, first job, first raise, bonus, life change, or an **annual plan review**—rather than an abstract distant future.

### 3.2 Tone Guidance Matrix (Page 3)

| Aim For | Why It Works | Avoid |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Realistic** | Acknowledges the actual world Gen Z is navigating. | Overly optimistic reassurance like *"don't worry"* or *"everything will work out."* |
| **Supportive** | Keeps focus on practical help and clear next steps. | Blame, judgment, or implying employees are careless with money. |
| **Empowering** | Highlights what is actually within the employee's control. | Fear-based messages, apocalyptic framing, or pressure tactics. |
| **Clear** | Lowers cognitive effort and makes taking action easy. | Financial jargon, long explanations, or vague calls to action. |
| **Flexible** | Reflects diverse goals, timelines, and saving capacities. | One-size-fits-all messaging about a traditional retirement path. |

### 3.3 Exact Dos and Don'ts (Page 4)

| Do | Don't |
| :--- | :--- |
| Say: *"The future can feel uncertain. Here's one step you can take today."* | Say: *"Stop worrying about the future."* |
| Say: *"We understand that uncertainty about the future can feel stressful."* | Say: *"We as an organization feel the stress of an uncertain future."* *(Crucial boundary: empathize without claiming to share their lived experience!)* |
| Frame saving as building options, flexibility, and resilience. | Frame savings only in terms of retirement at 65. |
| Offer **one clear action per message** and state the benefit. | Ask employees to make several decisions at once. |
| Use plain language: *"automatic contributions"* and *"money from each pay."* | Use unexplained acronyms or technical plan language. |
| Show that even a small contribution builds a savings habit. | Suggest saving is only worthwhile if contributing a large amount. |
| Recognize Gen Z balances short-term needs against long-term goals. | Assume they are impulsive, uninformed, or disengaged. |
| Make trustworthy information easy to find. | Force employees to dig through long documents to find what matters. |

### 3.4 10-Point Pre-Send Message Checklist (Page 5)
*(Claude can use this directly as a system prompt rubric or automated UX copy linter!)*
1. Acknowledge uncertainty in a realistic, authentic way.
2. Avoid fear-based, apocalyptic, or manipulative language.
3. Frame saving as control, flexibility, or resilience.
4. Connect to an immediate moment or decision the employee may be facing.
5. Give **one clear next step**.
6. Make the action feel small enough to start now.
7. Explain the benefit in plain language.
8. Avoid judgment about employees' choices or priorities.
9. Use trusted channels and make reliable information easy to find.
10. Support **action**, not just awareness.

### 3.5 Before & After Messaging Examples (Page 6)

| Scenario | Don't Say (Old Way) | Do Say (Canada Life Gen Z Standard) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Enrolment reminder** | *"Start saving for retirement today so you can retire comfortably decades from now."* | *"The future can feel hard to plan for. Starting with even X% (the max) from each pay will give you options for whatever comes next. Review your contributions today."* |
| **Automatic contributions** | *"Set up automatic contributions so you don't fall behind on retirement savings."* | *"Want one less thing to think about? Set up automatic contributions so you can build your savings over time. Start with the max and then adjust if the amount doesn't work for you."* |
| **First raise or bonus** | *"Use your raise to increase your retirement contribution."* | *"Getting a bonus or a raise feels good! Make that feeling last by putting a small part of it toward your workplace savings before adding it to your regular spending."* |
| **Long-term saving** | *"You need to think about retirement now, even if it feels far away."* | *"Saving isn't only about retirement. It gives you a cushion so you don't get buried in debt with an unexpected expense. And it gives you options later — like using it towards a strong financial foundation."* |
| **Plan education webinar** | *"Join our session to learn everything about your group retirement plan."* | *"Join a short session on 3 small steps you can take to make your workplace savings and retirement plan work for you. You can also watch a micro-learning video and learn practical actions you can use right away to work towards your own unique goals."* |

9. We explain how savings can support flexibility.

## 6. Synthesis: How All 4 Documents Connect Into Product Features

When Claude designs features, schemas, or UI flows for our hackathon app, combine all 4 PDFs into one cohesive system rather than treating them separately:

1. **Psychology + Problem Diagnosis (from PDF 1 — *The Disappearing Horizon*):**
   * Target the **retention & uncertainty gap** (12% / 42% higher RRSP withdrawals, 74 min/day doomscrolling, 1 in 8 existential outlook).
   * Build around **"soft-saving"**, **dual-bucket flexibility** (TFSA for short-term cushion + RRSP/employer match for long-term resilience), **1–3 year plan reviews**, and **small wins** (1% start, \$20/paycheque compounding, employer match capture).
2. **Copywriting, AI Coach & Behavioral Nudges (from PDF 2 — *Communications Guide*):**
   * Enforce the **10-Point Message Checklist** and **Tone Matrix** on every UI screen, push notification, and LLM coach response.
