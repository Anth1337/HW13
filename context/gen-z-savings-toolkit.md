## Overview: How the toolkit fits together

The toolkit is written for **employers and plan sponsors** (HR, benefits and total rewards teams) who offer group retirement and savings plans in Canada. It walks through a logical sequence:

| Module | Purpose | Core question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The Gen Z engagement context | Understand the problem | Why are Gen Z employees not engaging with workplace savings? |
| 2. Assess your current state | Diagnose | Where does our organization stand today? |
| 3. Implementation and tools | Act on communications | How do we talk to Gen Z, and with what tools? |
| 4. Considerations for plan design | Act on plan structure | How should the plan itself be designed? |
| 5. Moments that matter | Time the action | When should we intervene? |
| 6. Measure and improve | Close the loop | How do we know it's working? |
| Resources and downloads | Library | Where are all the materials? |

**The central thesis:** Gen Z's engagement gap is driven by **relevance, not awareness**. They know saving matters, but workplace plans feel disconnected from their immediate priorities and their uncertain view of the future. The strongest driver of engagement is **agency**, meaning the feeling that "I know what I can do next."

## Module 1 – The Gen Z engagement context

### The challenge

Many workplace retirement and savings plans were designed around traditional assumptions:

- Stable career paths
- Long-term planning
- Retirement as the primary goal
- Traditional financial decision-making

None of these assumptions reliably hold for Gen Z.

### The engagement gap

Many younger employees understand that saving is important. The challenge is that workplace retirement and savings plans can feel disconnected from their immediate priorities, financial realities and concerns about the future. When saving feels disconnected from what matters today, people are less likely to act.

This means engagement challenges are often driven **more by relevance than awareness**. The real question is whether employees see workplace savings as meaningful, practical and useful in their lives today, not just whether they understand the benefits of saving.

### What makes Gen Z different?

#### 1. They entered the workforce during uncertainty

- Youth unemployment sits at **14%**.
- Entry-level jobs are down **29%**.
- Only **45%** currently hold a single full-time role.
- Workers change roles every **1.1 years** on average.

#### 2. They think about the future differently

Canada Life's research showed Gen Z's savings behaviours are the result of several contributing factors:

**Hyperfocused on threats**
Gen Z is starting their careers amid global economic, climate and geopolitical uncertainty. They're bombarded with negative messaging and are more focused on potential dangers than on long-term planning. The risks feel real, immediate and overwhelming. Compared to other generations, they're:

- **70% more likely to think**, and **140% more likely to talk**, about the end of humanity several times a week.

**Averse to uncertainty**
They don't make decisions that hinge on the future of the world being predictable.

- **4.4% lower probability** of setting up automatic contributions toward long-term savings **for each additional hour spent doomscrolling daily**.

**Triggered by fear into "short-termism"**
They're feeling anxious or overwhelmed, so they focus on immediate concerns and avoid long-term planning, even when planning would help them. When people feel the future is unstable, they naturally discount it more heavily.

- **4 percentage point decrease** in the likelihood of holding long-term savings products from thinking about the end of the world.

#### 3. They are saving differently

Gen Z is saving, but they're also accessing their savings differently. According to Canada Life's internal data (2024 Canada Life claims data), Gen Zs withdraw from RRSPs:

- **12% more than Gen Y** at the same age
- **42% more than Gen X** at the same age

### Why does Gen Z saving matter for Canada?

If Gen Zs aren't saving for retirement, lower early-life investing has long-term consequences, including:

- Limiting compounding and capital growth
- Greater reliance on public assistance
- Reduced spending power in retirement
- Delayed retirement

### What this means for employers and their workforce

If Gen Zs continue to under-save, the risk for employers isn't just a future retirement issue. It's a broader **workforce well-being and productivity** issue. It can show up in the workplace in two ways.

**Fewer savings means more financial stress and reduced productivity.**
Financial stress consistently ranks as the top source of stress for Canadians, and that stress comes to work. It can affect productivity and engagement on the job.

**Increased disability risk and cost.**
Canada Life's research shows that mental illness accounts for approximately **30% of short- and long-term disability claims** but represents about **70% of workplace disability costs**. Financial stress, including from a lack of savings, might contribute to the mental health challenges that could lead an employee to take a mental health disability leave.

Reduced savings isn't solely a retirement issue; it's also a workforce wellness issue. Employees with stronger financial foundations tend to experience less stress, better mental health and greater resilience. These outcomes benefit the entire workforce, employees and employers alike, through improved productivity, attendance and engagement.

### What does this mean for workplace savings?

Given Gen Z's high turnover (an average of just 1.1 years with an employer) and the fact that only 45% hold a single full-time role, many have limited opportunities to fully benefit from employer-sponsored savings programs. Short tenures can:

- Reduce eligibility for workplace plans
- Limit the time available to build meaningful balances
- Increase the likelihood of withdrawing savings when changing jobs

Put together, these factors create a significant risk of retirement savings shortfalls over time.

### Employer programs are a key part of the solution

All of this makes employer programs an essential part of supporting Gen Z's financial security. The combination of **size, instability and mobility** in Gen Z makes them a critical demographic for employers looking to support financial resilience, improve retention and modernize plan offerings.

### It's a solvable problem

While there are obvious challenges, there's an equally big opportunity.

Younger employees **aren't disengaged; they're looking for answers**. They want:

- Clear, honest acknowledgment of their reality
- Small, achievable steps that help them feel more in control
- Practical ways to make progress in a world that feels increasingly uncertain

Using research, Canada Life identified the critical psychological insights for addressing this problem. Before starting, an organization needs to understand where it currently stands to determine which tactics will work best (Module 2).

## Module 2 – Assess your current state

### Where does your organization stand today?

Before making changes, it's important to understand where the current approach is already delivering value and where it can evolve to meet Gen Z's changing needs.

### Gen Z engagement self-assessment

Participation rates, contribution behaviours and communication effectiveness can show where a Gen Z engagement strategy is working and where there may be opportunities to improve. The self-assessment (downloadable PDF, 171 KB) helps employers review:

- Current communications
- Engagement approach
- Plan experience

It turns those signals into a practical starting point by showing where the current approach already supports Gen Z engagement, and where there may be gaps across **communications, savings relevance and the plan experience**.

### Interpreting your results

No matter what the results, the fact that an organization is thinking about this and working on solutions means it's ahead of most organizations.

| If most answers are... | Focus on... |
|---|---|
| **Yes** | Testing and optimization |
| **Partially** | Communications and moments that matter |
| **No** | Enrolment, communications and contribution behaviours |

The assessment simply gives more insight into how to take action with the tools and resources in the following modules.

### Where should you start?

Use the assessment results to focus efforts:

- You don't need to improve everything at once.
- Start with **1 communication, 1 employee moment or 1 engagement challenge**.

The next module helps identify the highest-impact opportunities.

## Module 3 – Implementation and tools

Before getting into tools and communication types, it's important to understand the communication goals and principles that lead to success.

### Agency is the key to Gen Z employee connection

The research found that employees are more likely to engage when communications:

1. **Acknowledge uncertainty**
2. **Create a sense of control**
3. **Reduce ambiguity**
4. **Provide realistic next steps**

> The strongest driver of engagement wasn't optimism. It was **agency**: the feeling that "I know what I can do next."

### How to engage Gen Z employees

| Goal | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Get them started** | Approach savings conversations in an empathetic, transparent, authentic, consistent way that offers clear action. |
| **Build their trust** | Engage them in relevant ways that connect to their values. |
| **Ease their fears** | Turn their worries into empowerment through guidance and education. |

### What you can do to help Gen Z employees

**Reframe**
Position information and make content applicable to their reality without changing the underlying facts.

**Build choice architecture**
Introduce payroll deductions and employer matching, and give employees ways to contribute and save in products that flex with their financial priorities. For example, make products like TFSAs available.

**Employ emotionally aware communication**
Adjust messages so they feel supportive, respectful and easy to act on.

**Give them small wins**
For many Gen Z employees, major financial milestones may feel out of reach due to rising costs and delayed entry into full-time work. Employers can build confidence and positive financial habits by celebrating smaller savings achievements along the way. Examples of small wins:

- Enrolling in the plan
- Making a first contribution
- Increasing contributions by 1%
- Maximizing the employer match
- Building an emergency fund while saving for retirement

**Support flexible saving**
Allow employees to save in ways that reflect their current priorities. This may include:

- Offering a mix of savings options (e.g., TFSAs and retirement accounts)
- Encouraging smaller starting contributions
- Connecting savings to personal life goals
- Promoting tools that help employees balance competing priorities such as emergency savings, debt repayment, homeownership and long-term financial security

**Let them save their way**
Consider how the program can give employees greater flexibility to save in ways that align with their current financial priorities:

- Enable access to a mix of savings vehicles such as TFSAs alongside retirement accounts
- Frame retirement savings in terms of **future life goals** rather than distant retirement dates
- Allow employees to start with smaller contribution amounts
- Make members aware of tools that help balance competing objectives such as emergency savings, student debt, homeownership and long-term financial security

**Make it easy to find credible information**
Gen Z has no shortage of financial content, but not all of it is accurate, balanced or applicable to their situation. Employers can play a valuable role by connecting employees with credible, easy-to-understand resources. Employers can support employees by:

- Sharing short, engaging educational content
- Providing timely guidance at key financial milestones
- Promoting trusted financial resources and professionals
- Highlighting credible tools, calculators and projections
- Focusing communications on **one clear action**

### Contributions calculator

**Purpose:** See how the size of contributions to a group plan can affect retirement income.

#### Inputs

| Field | Type |
|---|---|
| How old are you? | Age |
| At what age do you want to retire? | Age |
| How are you contributing to retirement today? | Choice: **a percentage of annual income** or **a dollar amount** |
| What's your annual income before taxes? | $ |
| What percentage of your annual income are you contributing? (or dollar amount) | % or $ |
| Pre-retirement rate of return | % |

After calculating, the user can change contributions to see what a difference saving more makes ("Want to save more?").

#### Four tips to save more using your group plan(s)

1. **Start investing now.** The sooner you start, the more opportunity your money has to grow.
2. **Contribute as much as you can** to make the most of any amount your employer may match.
3. **Increase your contributions when you can** (e.g., when you get a raise).
4. **Contribute more frequently** if your plan allows.

#### Assumptions

- The estimated annual income is based on a **complete withdrawal of the savings in the retirement year**. It isn't a payout annuity.
- If the contribution is a **percentage**, the amount is adjusted proportionately with salary increases.
- If the contribution is a **dollar amount**, it stays the same for the entire contribution period.
- Annual contributions are made at the **beginning of the calendar year**.
- Value of existing retirement savings = **0** (the age entered is the year of the first contribution).
- Annual salary increase = **2.5%**.
- Years in retirement = **25** (retirement income stops after 25 years).
- Post-retirement rate of return = **5%**.

#### How the math works (implied by the assumptions)

Let \(A\) = current age, \(R\) = retirement age, \(n = R - A\) years of contributions, \(S_0\) = starting salary, \(p\) = contribution percentage, \(r\) = pre-retirement return, \(g = 2.5\%\) salary growth.

- Contribution in year \(t\) (percentage mode): \(C_t = p \cdot S_0 (1+g)^t\)
- Contribution in year \(t\) (dollar mode): \(C_t = C\) (constant)
- Balance at retirement (contributions at the start of each year):

\[
B = \sum_{t=0}^{n-1} C_t (1+r)^{\,n-t}
\]

- Annual retirement income over 25 years at a 5% post-retirement return (annuity-style drawdown of the balance):

\[
I = B \cdot \frac{0.05}{1 - (1.05)^{-25}}
\]

#### Disclaimer

This calculator is for information purposes only and shows how contributions to a group retirement and savings plan can affect retirement income. It's not a prediction or guarantee of future investment performance or of what will be received as retirement income. Retirement income may be higher or lower based on market conditions, investment performance and legislative changes.

### Impact of early withdrawals calculator

**Purpose:** See how making early withdrawals from a group retirement and savings plan affects future retirement income.

#### Inputs

| Field | Type |
|---|---|
| How old are you? | Age |
| At what age do you want to retire? | Age |
| What is your current total retirement savings? | $ |
| What is your annual rate of return? | % |
| How much are you looking to withdraw now? | $ |

#### What it shows

The difference between what current savings would grow to by retirement with and without the withdrawal. With \(W\) withdrawn today, \(n\) years to retirement and annual return \(r\), the lost future value is roughly:

\[
\text{Lost value at retirement} = W (1+r)^{n}
\]

This makes the cost of compounding visible: a withdrawal at a young age costs far more than its face value by retirement. (In Canada, RRSP withdrawals are also subject to withholding tax and are added to taxable income, and the contribution room is not restored.)

## Module 4 – Considerations for plan design

Improving engagement with Gen Z requires **more than effective communication**. Clear, relevant messaging is important, but meaningful engagement also depends on whether **plan design** reflects how younger employees save, work and think about the future.

As Gen Z becomes a larger share of the workforce, employers have an opportunity to reduce barriers to participation, increase flexibility and make workplace savings feel relevant from day one.

### The opportunity

The most effective plan designs **balance behavioural guidance with flexibility**. Employers can create a savings experience that reflects how Gen Z actually lives and saves by:

- Reducing eligibility delays
- Maintaining strong defaults
- Supporting multiple savings goals
- Simplifying decision-making

The goal is **not to abandon retirement planning**. It's to help younger employees take action now while building confidence in their future, whatever that future may look like.

### Remove barriers to getting started

Because Gen Z changes jobs more frequently than previous generations, long eligibility or waiting periods can mean they spend a significant portion of their tenure unable to participate.

**Consider:**

- Reducing or eliminating eligibility waiting periods where permitted
- A **base employer contribution** (e.g., 2–3%) with matching contributions above that level
- Behavioural nudges such as **automatic enrolment** and **contribution escalation**

**Important considerations:**

- Assess how withdrawal restrictions may affect the employee experience.
- Consider the impact of legislative and plan-specific rules governing access to savings and retirement funds.

The easier it is to start saving, the more likely employees are to establish positive habits early.

### Won't flexible programs lead to more withdrawals and less retirement savings?

**Not necessarily.** Focusing only on withdrawals may overlook the broader savings behaviour employers are trying to influence.

The question may not be "How do we prevent every withdrawal?" but rather **"How do we encourage more employees to begin saving and stay engaged with saving over the long term?"**

For many younger employees, flexibility is an **entry point** into strong savings habits, building confidence and ultimately accumulating greater wealth over their lifetime.

### Support today's needs and tomorrow's goals

Many younger employees think about financial well-being differently than previous generations. Retirement remains important, but it's only **one of several competing financial priorities**.

Rather than asking employees to choose between today's needs and tomorrow's security, programs can support both. A balanced approach:

- **Employee contributions** can be directed to a TFSA, a First Home Savings Account (FHSA) or other personal savings accounts.
- **Employer contributions** continue to flow to an RRSP or pension plan.

This lets employees address immediate financial priorities while still building retirement savings through employer support. The goal isn't to replace retirement savings with short-term savings, but to acknowledge that employees manage multiple goals simultaneously while employer contributions reinforce the plan's core retirement objective.

### Put automatic enrolment to work

Flexibility makes workplace savings more relevant, but **automatic enrolment makes savings happen**.

> Note: The permissibility of automatic enrolment depends on the type of plan and the applicable jurisdiction.

Flexible design doesn't eliminate the behavioural barriers that stop people from getting started: **procrastination, competing priorities and decision fatigue**. Automatic enrolment remains one of the most effective plan design features, helping employees take the critical first step while preserving the flexibility to adjust later.

Combine flexibility with strong defaults by:

1. Automatically enrolling eligible employees
2. Defaulting contributions to a retirement savings product
3. Allowing employees to redirect their own contributions later if another option better meets their needs

This creates a simple starting point while preserving flexibility and control.

### Highlight flexibility

Highlight optional savings products available through the plan provider, giving employees more flexibility at a reduced cost to the employer.

For example, a plan may only include an RRSP, while employees can still access a TFSA through Canada Life's **Freedom Financial™ NextStep™** plan. Making employees aware of these options helps them find solutions that fit their needs without redesigning the entire plan.

## Module 5 – Moments that matter

### Engagement happens in moments

Messaging should run through all employee touchpoints, but research shows **behavioural change happens when employees are making decisions**.

### A principle that applies to every moment

Employees are more likely to act when communications:

1. **Acknowledge the reality** of what Gen Z is experiencing, and the confluence of social, economic and environmental factors shaping how they view the future
2. **Make the savings benefit relevant**
3. **Present one simple next step**

### The four moments

#### 1. Enrolment and onboarding

| | |
|---|---|
| **Challenge** | Employees delay joining. |
| **Why it breaks down** | The future feels distant and enrolling feels easy to postpone. |
| **Recommended action** | Start with a simple enrolment message that makes getting started easy. |

What you can do:

- Simplify internal enrolment processes
- Use clear calls to action
- Focus on flexibility and options
- Reinforce employer matching

#### 2. Contribution decisions

| | |
|---|---|
| **Challenge** | Employees contribute too little or fail to increase contributions. |
| **Why it breaks down** | Saving competes with immediate priorities like rent or not missing out on social events. |
| **Recommended action** | Establish a consistent communication touchpoint that illustrates small actions and wins. |

What you can do:

- Promote getting started with payroll deductions, no matter how small
- Encourage employees to take advantage of matching (if available)
- Focus on small wins, such as a short-term savings goal of $1,000 or $5,000

#### 3. Financial pressure moments

| | |
|---|---|
| **Challenge** | Employees reduce contributions or disengage. |
| **Why it breaks down** | Stress from global risk or existential dread creates avoidance. |
| **Recommended action** | Provide timely education and clear actions during uncertainty, such as adjusting contribution levels, reviewing investment strategy or maintaining even small contributions to stay on track. |

What employers can do:

- Acknowledge the situation
- Offer options
- Encourage **adjustment rather than disengagement**

#### 4. Ongoing engagement

| | |
|---|---|
| **Challenge** | Employees forget about the plan. |
| **Why it breaks down** | Communications lose relevance. |
| **Recommended action** | Build recurring engagement touchpoints. |

What employers can do:

- Use bite-sized content across different media, including micro-learning videos and written communications like email
- Connect savings to real-life goals

## Module 6 – Measure and improve

Success isn't just awareness. It's helping Gen Z **take action** and shift their financial mindset and habits.

### Key metrics to track

| Category | Metrics |
|---|---|
| **Participation** | Enrolment rates; time it takes employees to enrol; participation by age group |
| **Contributions** | Average contribution rate; employer matching adoption; contribution increases |
| **Engagement** | Email open and click-through rates; resource downloads |

### Create a review rhythm

**Track monthly:**

- Enrolment
- Email performance
- Contribution activity

**Review quarterly:**

- Behaviour trends
- Engagement gaps
- Feedback and survey results

### Test and learn

- Retirement language versus flexibility language
- Different calls to action
- Different email timing

### Signs your strategy is working

- More employees enrolling shortly after joining
- Higher participation rates among younger employees
- Increasing employee contributions over time
- Greater use of educational resources and tools
- Improved employee confidence
- Greater understanding of the value of the workplace savings program

### Wealth and health are interconnected

For Gen Z, financial wellness and mental wellness are closely linked. Building savings isn't only about preparing for the future; it's also about reducing financial anxiety, increasing resilience and creating a greater sense of control and well-being **today**.

For employees who may be struggling, benefits like an **Employee Assistance Program (EAP)** or other mental health supports can help reduce stress, improve well-being and reframe their thinking to give them a sense of control.

### Final takeaway

Gen Z is often defined by the challenges they face, but they're equally defined by their **adaptability, resilience, desire for purpose and commitment to growth**. The organizations that succeed in attracting, retaining and engaging them are the ones that communicate in ways that feel **relevant, authentic and actionable**.

By acknowledging employees' realities, creating relevance and making it easier to act, employers can build deeper trust and stronger connections with this generation of talent. These principles improve savings outcomes today, and their impact extends further: supporting engagement, retention, well-being and a workplace culture where employees feel understood, valued and empowered.

The future of work is being shaped by Gen Z. Organizations that invest in understanding and engaging them today will be better positioned to help them thrive tomorrow and in the years to come.

## Glossary

- **RRSP (Registered Retirement Savings Plan):** Canadian tax-deferred retirement account. Contributions are tax-deductible; withdrawals are taxed as income, and withdrawn contribution room is not restored.
- **TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account):** Canadian account where investment growth and withdrawals are tax-free; withdrawn amounts are re-added to contribution room the next year. Flexible for short- and medium-term goals.
- **FHSA (First Home Savings Account):** Canadian account for first-time home buyers, combining a tax deduction on contributions with tax-free qualifying withdrawals for a home purchase.
- **Pension plan:** Employer-sponsored retirement plan (defined benefit or defined contribution).
- **Employer matching:** Employer contributes an amount tied to what the employee contributes (e.g., 50% of employee contributions up to 6% of salary).
- **Base employer contribution:** A fixed employer contribution (e.g., 2–3% of salary) made regardless of employee contributions.
- **Automatic enrolment:** Eligible employees are enrolled by default and can opt out or adjust.
- **Contribution escalation:** Automatic, scheduled increases to contribution rates (e.g., +1% per year).
- **Choice architecture:** Designing how options are presented (defaults, order, framing) to make good decisions easier.
- **Short-termism:** Prioritizing immediate concerns over long-term planning, often driven by anxiety about the future.
- **Doomscrolling:** Continuously consuming negative news or social media content.
- **Agency:** The feeling of knowing what one can do next; identified by the research as the strongest driver of Gen Z engagement.
- **EAP (Employee Assistance Program):** Employer-provided confidential support services, often including mental health counselling.
- **Freedom Financial™ NextStep™:** Canada Life plan that lets employees access a TFSA even when their workplace plan only includes an RRSP.
